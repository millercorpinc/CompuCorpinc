# Market and Positioning

## Source and status

- Core 10–150-employee customer model: established business baseline from Startup-project reconstruction
- Chicago segmentation expansion: research hypothesis developed 2026-08-19
- Detailed evidence: `../research/chicago-market/README.md`
- Decision rule: research below does not approve a new permanent vertical or microbusiness product without customer and commercial evidence

## Initial ideal customer hypothesis

Organizations with approximately 10–150 employees that use Microsoft 365 and have outgrown informal technology operations.

Broader practical range: approximately 1–200 users.

This remains the primary operating baseline.

## Research-informed refinement

Chicago market research indicates that **employee count should be treated as a useful size signal rather than the sole qualification gate**.

A much smaller business can still be attractive when it combines:

- meaningful receipts/revenue;
- high value per user or device;
- sensitive client, health, legal, financial, project, or intellectual-property data;
- meaningful wire, ACH, payment, or transaction exposure;
- Microsoft 365/cloud dependence;
- several important business applications;
- external contractors or collaborators;
- customer, insurer, professional, or regulatory security requirements;
- high downtime cost; and
- no experienced internal technology/security owner.

The research therefore supports testing two related but economically distinct markets:

### Core advisory market

Small employer organizations—especially professional, regulated, knowledge-intensive, and field-service firms—that can support a paid assessment, Foundation/remediation work, and recurring governance.

Current strongest size hypothesis: approximately 10–49 users, while continuing to test smaller and larger accounts.

### Productized microbusiness market — research hypothesis

Serious owner-operated and very small firms may have meaningful technology/security exposure despite zero or very few employees.

This market should **not** receive a miniature version of the full consulting engagement by default. It may require a separate standardized security/continuity baseline with tighter scope, automation, channel acquisition, and lighter recurring governance.

No microbusiness offer is approved yet.

## Common conditions

- No experienced internal technology executive
- One IT generalist, owner-administered environment, or legacy outsourced provider
- Inconsistent identity, device, collaboration, and data controls
- Manual workflows and disconnected systems
- Weak documentation and unclear ownership
- Increasing customer, insurer, professional, or compliance requirements
- Need for modernization without a large transformation program
- Existing technical support but limited architecture/governance

## Researched Chicago vertical hypotheses

The current launch tracks remain appropriate for comparative validation:

1. accounting, CPA, tax, bookkeeping, and advisory practices;
2. law firms and other regulated/confidential professional firms;
3. healthcare practices and outpatient providers; and
4. construction, contractors, field services, property, and transaction-heavy businesses.

Additional selective markets for later testing include:

- finance and insurance;
- architecture, engineering, design, and specialist consulting; and
- appropriately sized nonprofits and associations.

### Why accounting/tax is particularly strong

Research identifies an unusually direct external-security trigger: tax professionals are required to maintain a Written Information Security Plan, including safeguards and service-provider considerations. The Illinois professional ecosystem is also large.

### Why legal is particularly strong

Small law firms combine direct partner/owner purchasing authority, confidential information, payment/trust exposure, and comparatively limited security maturity. The 2–9-lawyer band deserves explicit testing.

### Why healthcare is strong but operationally harder

Small practices have meaningful security/regulatory pressure and high downtime cost, but healthcare data access, BAAs, clinical systems, and support expectations increase delivery and contractual complexity.

### Why construction/field services is different

Employee count can substantially understate business scale because contractors coordinate subcontractors and distributed project teams. Project value, external users, field devices, payment workflows, and application complexity should be included in qualification.

### Why real estate needs hard filtering

The market is numerically large and transaction/wire fraud is highly salient, but many individual agents have limited budgets. Focus should be on teams, brokerages, property managers, commercial operators, and high-producing owner-operated businesses unless a low-cost productized offer proves viable.

## Account qualification dimensions

Build qualification around:

- receipts/revenue band;
- users and identities;
- managed devices;
- industry and business model;
- sensitive data;
- payment/wire exposure;
- Microsoft/cloud dependence;
- number and criticality of business applications;
- regulatory/customer/insurance requirements;
- subcontractor/external collaborator access;
- downtime consequence;
- current IT maturity;
- owner personally administering technology;
- incumbent provider capability; and
- active buying trigger.

A structured 100-point research score is defined in `../research/chicago-market/03-SEGMENT-OPPORTUNITY-MATRIX.md`; do not treat its weights as validated until prospect/pilot evidence exists.

## Competitive implication from Chicago research

The Chicago managed-IT market is crowded. Generic “managed IT,” cybersecurity, Microsoft 365, help desk, and vCIO claims are already common.

Priority differentiation hypotheses to test:

- independent assessment/remediation that can coexist with an incumbent MSP;
- architecture/governance for organizations that are technically supported but not well governed;
- evidence-oriented security baselines tied to client, insurer, professional, or regulatory pressure;
- security plus workflow/integration modernization; and
- a separate productized microbusiness baseline below conventional full-MSP economics.

See `../research/chicago-market/06-COMPETITIVE-LANDSCAPE-CHICAGO.md`.

## Differentiation

- Senior founder involvement
- Strategy before tooling
- Security and documentation built into delivery
- Microsoft depth without Microsoft-only dogma
- Cross-system automation and integration
- Repeatable architecture and service standards
- Partner ecosystem for specialized outcomes
- Recurring ownership after implementation
- Ability to coexist with existing support where appropriate
- Evidence and accountable ownership rather than a tool-only security story

## Validation needs

The earlier minimum of ten buyer interviews is no longer sufficient for the expanded segmentation question.

Research plan:

- conduct approximately 30–40 structured target-buyer interviews;
- deliberately sample owner-operated/1–4, 5–9, 10–49, and 50+ user bands;
- measure current technology/provider spend;
- identify active buying triggers;
- compare current provider conditions;
- test willingness to buy an independent assessment without switching MSPs;
- test assessment-first versus implementation-first motion;
- test paid Foundation/remediation willingness;
- test whether a microbusiness baseline can support acceptable CAC and gross margin;
- measure vertical referral/channel density; and
- determine whether a vertical focus improves acquisition and repeatability.
