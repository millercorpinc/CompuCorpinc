# Nonemployer Economy Deep Dive

## Source and status

- **Status:** measured national/metro facts, derived calculations, and explicitly labeled Chicago scenarios
- **Date:** 2026-08-19
- **Key warning:** “nonemployer” is a payroll classification, not a synonym for gig worker, side hustle, low-income owner, or informal business.

## Definition

Census Nonemployer Statistics cover businesses with **no paid employees** that are subject to federal income tax and meet minimum annual-receipt thresholds. The standard threshold is at least $1,000 of receipts, except construction, where the threshold is $1.

Source: U.S. Census Bureau, 2023 Nonemployer Statistics: https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/nonemployer-statistics.html

A nonemployer establishment can therefore be:

- an individual proprietorship;
- an S corporation;
- a C corporation/other corporation;
- a partnership; or
- another taxable owner-operated structure represented in the source data.

The population can include low-revenue side work **and** substantial professional or owner-operated businesses.

## National scale in 2023

Census 2023 NES reports:

- **30,427,808** nonemployer establishments; and
- **$1.753 trillion** in total receipts.

Derived arithmetic average receipts are approximately **$57,600 per establishment**.

This average is not representative of a typical establishment because the distribution is highly skewed.

Census table: https://data.census.gov/table/NONEMP2023.NS2300NONEMP

### Commercial implication

The majority of nonemployer establishments are not plausible buyers of a $6,000–$18,000 technology implementation.

That does **not** make the segment irrelevant. A small percentage of an extremely large population can still create a substantial target universe, especially when the target is narrowed to professional, regulated, transaction-heavy, or project-based industries.

## Chicago scale

The 2022 common-year SBA profile reports **898,077** Chicago metro nonemployer establishments.

A secondary synthesis of 2023 Census data reports approximately **916,466**.

The appropriate conclusion is not “Chicago has 916,466 prospects.” The appropriate conclusion is:

> Chicago has an unusually large owner-operated business substrate from which a high-value tail can be identified.

## Chicago receipt-band scenario

### Method

Standard NES does not provide the same receipt-size-class distribution for the Chicago metro that it provides nationally. Therefore, the following calculation applies the **national 2023 receipt distribution** to the reported Chicago 2023 nonemployer count solely as a scenario.

It is **not an observed Chicago distribution**.

| Modeled receipt threshold | National share | Chicago scenario count |
|---|---:|---:|
| $100k+ | 13.25% | ~121,400 |
| $250k+ | 4.67% | ~42,800 |
| $500k+ | 1.74% | ~15,900 |
| $1M+ | 0.385% | ~3,500 |

The scenario is useful for scale intuition: even if only a small single-digit percentage of Chicago nonemployers have substantial receipts, that can still mean tens of thousands of organizations.

Do not use these modeled counts in external marketing, financial forecasts, or investor materials as measured Chicago facts.

## Legal-form distribution nationally

Census 2023 nonemployer data report approximately:

| Legal form | Establishments | Receipts |
|---|---:|---:|
| Individual proprietorships | 26.28M | ~$1.061T |
| Partnerships | 2.27M | ~$378.2B |
| S corporations | 1.41M | ~$234.4B |
| C corporations/other corporate | 0.46M | ~$79.8B |

### Interpretation

The nonemployer universe is overwhelmingly proprietorships by count, but partnerships and corporations account for a disproportionate amount of receipts relative to their count.

**Hypothesis for qualification:** legal form may be a useful but imperfect lead-scoring signal. An S corporation, professional entity, or partnership with no payroll may be more likely than a very low-receipt sole proprietor to support professional technology spending.

Legal form should never be used alone; it can reflect tax/entity preferences rather than operating complexity.

## What the largest categories really mean

### Transportation & warehousing

This is the largest nonemployer category in Chicago, but count does not equal serviceability. Independent drivers and owner-operators may have limited business technology outside mobile applications, payments, dispatch, and accounting.

Potential higher-value subsegments include freight brokers, logistics consultants, specialized transportation operators, and businesses coordinating subcontracted drivers/vehicles.

**Recommendation:** do not treat the category as a priority until 6-digit NAICS and receipt data isolate higher-complexity operators.

### Professional, scientific & technical services

This is the most strategically interesting broad nonemployer pool because it can include:

- legal services;
- accounting/tax/bookkeeping;
- consulting;
- architecture;
- engineering;
- design;
- computer/professional services;
- marketing/communications; and
- specialized scientific/technical practices.

These firms often monetize knowledge rather than physical inventory. That can produce high value per identity/device and heavy dependence on email, documents, SaaS, cloud storage, client data, and professional reputation.

### Construction

Zero payroll is especially misleading in construction because businesses can coordinate independent trades and subcontractors. A contractor may have one owner on payroll, several software systems, multiple field devices, dozens of external collaborators, significant project revenue, insurance/customer obligations, and payment-fraud exposure.

Potential qualification variables:

- annual project volume;
- active projects;
- subcontractor count;
- office + field identities/devices;
- Procore/Buildertrend/Autodesk/Bluebeam or similar systems;
- payment and change-order workflow;
- document/photo volume;
- cyber-insurance requirements; and
- public/enterprise customer security requirements.

### Healthcare/social assistance

A one-owner practice can still have:

- EHR;
- patient scheduling;
- telehealth;
- payment processing;
- protected health information;
- mobile devices;
- Microsoft 365/email;
- backups;
- third-party integrations; and
- HIPAA Security Rule obligations.

This is a strong example of why technology risk can be largely independent of payroll count.

### Real estate

A solo broker or small team can handle high-dollar transactions while operating on a very small internal technology stack. That creates a specific security problem: email account compromise, credential theft, transaction impersonation, and wire fraud.

The offer should therefore be narrow and concrete rather than a generic vCIO proposition.

## Why nonemployer technology economics differ

### Conventional high-touch consulting problem

At the current Foundation Core hypothesis of approximately $6,000–$9,000, many nonemployers would need to allocate an implausibly large percentage of annual receipts to one IT project.

For example:

- at $50,000 receipts, a $6,000 project equals 12% of gross receipts;
- at $100,000 receipts, it equals 6%;
- at $250,000 receipts, it equals 2.4%;
- at $500,000 receipts, it equals 1.2%;
- at $1 million receipts, it equals 0.6%.

These ratios do not include recurring fees and do not account for profit margin.

This illustrates why **receipt threshold and risk intensity must be part of qualification**.

### Potential productized model

A viable owner-operated offer would likely need to reduce both price and delivery effort through:

- a tightly supported Microsoft 365 license/configuration pattern;
- pre-flight questionnaire and automated evidence collection;
- standardized MFA/Conditional Access/admin controls;
- endpoint encryption/patch/security baseline;
- domain/email authentication;
- backup/recovery baseline;
- SaaS/admin inventory;
- security/recovery documentation;
- a small incident-response/wire-verification runbook; and
- annual or semiannual governance review.

A product like this should be tested independently from the full Foundation because its margin model, sales channel, scope, and customer expectations are different.

## Nonemployer archetypes worth testing

### High priority

- solo/small tax preparer or CPA practice;
- solo/small law practice;
- therapist, psychologist, physician, dental or other independent health practice where applicable;
- architecture/engineering/design principal with project collaborators;
- high-revenue independent consultant handling confidential client data;
- general contractor or specialty contractor coordinating subcontractors;
- property manager or transaction-heavy real-estate professional;
- independent financial/insurance professional subject to customer-data obligations.

### Medium priority

- boutique marketing/creative agency with contractors;
- association-management consultant;
- high-revenue independent recruiter;
- specialty business-service provider;
- nonprofit consultant/fundraiser with client datasets.

### Low priority without additional evidence

- generic rideshare/delivery owner-operator;
- low-receipt side business;
- hobby-adjacent seller;
- simple cash/retail microbusiness with limited data/systems;
- owner who does not depend materially on cloud/email/business records.

## Qualification questions for a nonemployer prospect

1. What were approximate business receipts last year?
2. Is this the owner's primary business?
3. How many people—including contractors—regularly access company data or systems?
4. How many business-managed devices exist?
5. Which email/cloud platform is authoritative?
6. What client/customer information is stored?
7. Does the business move client or vendor money by wire/ACH?
8. Does it have cyber insurance?
9. Does any customer, insurer, regulator, or professional body require security controls?
10. What happens if email/files/core systems are unavailable for a day?
11. Who currently administers Microsoft 365, devices, domains, backups, and access?
12. When was access last reviewed?
13. Is MFA enforced everywhere material?
14. Are backups independently recoverable and tested?
15. What triggered the conversation now?

## Strategic decision test

The nonemployer opportunity should advance only if pilot evidence shows that the company can achieve:

- repeatable customer qualification;
- low customer-acquisition cost through trusted channels;
- narrow supported environments;
- strong gross margin at a materially lower ticket than the full Foundation;
- low post-project support burden;
- measurable security/continuity value; and
- a clear upgrade path as the customer grows.

If those conditions fail, the research still improves the core employer-market ICP by showing why employee count should not be a hard gate.
