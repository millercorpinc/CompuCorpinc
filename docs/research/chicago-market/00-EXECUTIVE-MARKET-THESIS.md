## Thesis in one sentence

Chicago contains a very large population of small and owner-operated businesses with meaningful technology, security, workflow, and governance exposure; the opportunity is not to serve all of them, but to distinguish **high-risk/high-value small organizations** from low-economic-intensity businesses and match each segment to an appropriately productized service model.

## What changed from the original customer model

The established company baseline emphasizes organizations with roughly 10–150 employees and a broader practical range of 1–200 users. That remains a strong core customer hypothesis.

The market research adds an important correction: **headcount alone is an incomplete proxy for technology need or economic attractiveness**.

A zero-payroll or two-person firm can still have:

- substantial business receipts;
- sensitive client, financial, health, legal, or design data;
- Microsoft 365, cloud storage, line-of-business applications, accounting, CRM, e-signature, and payment systems;
- contractual or regulatory security obligations;
- cyber-insurance requirements;
- significant wire-transfer or payment-fraud exposure;
- subcontractors and external collaborators;
- a high cost of downtime; and
- no internal technology leadership.

A 15-person low-margin operation with limited systems may therefore be a worse customer than a one-owner professional firm with $500,000 of receipts, valuable client records, a trust or operating account, and a cloud-dependent workflow.

## Chicago market structure

The SBA Office of Advocacy's 2025 Chicago-Naperville-Elgin profile reports approximately:

- **1,098,690 small businesses** on a common 2022 basis;
- **898,077 nonemployer establishments**;
- **177,996 employer firms with 1–19 employees**; and
- **22,617 employer firms with 20–499 employees**.

That means the small-business population is numerically dominated by organizations with **no paid employees**.

Source: U.S. Small Business Administration, Office of Advocacy, *2025 Small Business Profiles for Major Metropolitan Areas*, using Census SUSB/NES data: https://advocacy.sba.gov/2025/10/28/2025-small-business-profiles-for-major-metropolitan-areas/

A later 2023 nonemployer estimate reported from Census data places Chicago metro nonemployers around 916,000. Because the employer and nonemployer source years differ, the common-year SBA profile remains the preferred baseline for segment comparisons.

## The two-market hypothesis

### Market A — advisory-led small employers

**Working profile**

- commonly 5/10–150 users, with exceptions;
- professional, regulated, knowledge-intensive, project-based, or field-service work;
- meaningful Microsoft 365/cloud environment;
- little or no senior IT/security leadership;
- enough complexity and budget to value judgment, remediation, implementation, and recurring governance.

**Likely commercial path**

Technology and Security Baseline Assessment → Secure Workplace Foundation/remediation → automation/integration → recurring governance.

**Why it fits the established company**

This is where senior judgment, architecture, documentation, security, integration, and governance can carry enough economic value to avoid competing as a commodity help desk.

### Market B — serious owner-operated and microbusinesses

**Working profile**

- zero to roughly four employees/users, sometimes more;
- owner directly administers technology;
- financially meaningful business but too small for a conventional MSP minimum or high-touch fractional-CIO engagement;
- cloud/email/device dependence and material security or continuity exposure.

**Likely commercial path**

A highly standardized security/technology baseline → limited remediation → scheduled/annual governance or a light subscription.

**Required economics**

This cannot be a smaller copy of the full consulting service. It likely requires:

- narrow supported architecture;
- automation;
- strong prerequisites;
- fixed implementation patterns;
- limited customization;
- channel/referral acquisition;
- minimal ongoing help-desk scope; and
- lower-cost periodic governance.

This is a **separate product hypothesis**, not an approved expansion of the current service portfolio.

## Best initial vertical hypotheses

### Tier 1 — strongest first validation candidates

#### Accounting, tax, CPA, bookkeeping, and advisory practices

Why:

- highly sensitive taxpayer and financial data;
- tax professionals are expressly required to maintain a Written Information Security Plan regardless of firm size;
- IRS guidance tells tax professionals to assess risks, maintain safeguards, monitor/test them, and ensure service providers safeguard customer information;
- the Illinois CPA Society reports more than 20,000 accounting and finance professional members, with 42% of its membership in public practice;
- strong seasonality and business-continuity sensitivity;
- obvious referral adjacency to the company's own customers.

This segment has an unusually direct bridge between a technology/security assessment and a business/legal requirement.

#### Legal practices

Why:

- substantial solo/small-firm population;
- client confidentiality, document, email, trust-account, and wire exposure;
- direct owner/partner control of technology purchasing in smaller firms;
- cloud/remote work and e-filing are normal operating dependencies;
- ABA technology surveys show solo and small firms remain materially less mature on security controls than larger firms.

Risk: Chicago already has MSPs specializing in law firms, so the company must differentiate beyond generic “IT for lawyers.”

#### Healthcare practices and outpatient providers

Why:

- sensitive data and high downtime cost;
- formal HIPAA Security Rule risk-analysis and safeguard obligations;
- HHS publishes security guidance specifically for small providers and a Security Risk Assessment Tool for small/medium practices;
- ransomware and cyber-incident response are explicit HHS security topics;
- Chicago Medical Society says it represents more than 17,000 physicians.

Risk: BAAs, HIPAA boundaries, healthcare applications, and incident obligations increase delivery and contractual complexity.

### Tier 2 — high-potential, different buying logic

#### Construction, contractors, trades, and field services

Why:

- very large Chicago nonemployer and employer base;
- many firms coordinate subcontractors rather than W-2 employees, making employee count a particularly poor size measure;
- project documents, estimates, accounting, field devices, email, payments, and external collaboration create a real stack;
- Chicagoland AGC says its general-contractor members range from small specialist firms to national companies and collectively build more than $12 billion annually.

Best wedge may combine security with operational standardization, device/user lifecycle, file collaboration, vendor/subcontractor access, and workflow automation.

#### Real estate, brokerage, property, title-adjacent, and related professionals

Why:

- huge owner-operated population;
- payment/wire exposure is unusually salient;
- FBI and Illinois REALTORS repeatedly warn about compromised-email and transaction fraud;
- Chicago Association of REALTORS represents more than 16,000 members and offers direct advertising/sponsorship channels.

Risk: many individual agents have low or variable technology budgets. Target teams, brokerages, property managers, high-producing professionals, and transaction-heavy operators rather than generic agents.

### Tier 3 — attractive selective markets

- finance, insurance, lending, advisory, and other Safeguards-Rule-adjacent firms;
- architecture, engineering, and specialized consulting firms;
- associations and nonprofits with meaningful staff, grants, donor/client data, and Microsoft environments.

AIA Chicago alone reported 3,721 members in 2025, including a dedicated Small Firm Exchange. Chicagoland also has a very large nonprofit population; one April 2026 IRS Business Master File analysis identified roughly 42,000 active 501(c)(3) organizations across the 13-county region. These are real ecosystems but need tighter budget and technology-intensity filters before being treated as launch verticals.

## The qualification model should change

Use headcount as one input, not the gate.

A stronger target-account score should include:

| Factor | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Receipts / revenue band | Ability to fund professional work |
| Users / identities | Operating complexity and recurring-service base |
| Managed devices | Security and administration surface |
| Industry | Determines risk, regulation, workflow, and software |
| Sensitive data | Increases breach and contractual consequences |
| Payment / wire exposure | Increases BEC and fraud value |
| Microsoft/cloud dependence | Improves technical fit and business criticality |
| Business application count | Indicates integration/governance complexity |
| Regulatory/customer obligations | Creates external urgency |
| Cyber-insurance requirements | Creates evidence/control urgency |
| Subcontractor/external collaborator count | Indicates identity/data-sharing complexity |
| Downtime cost | Increases resilience value |
| Current IT maturity | Determines gap and implementation opportunity |
| Owner personally administering IT | Strong signal of senior-judgment gap |
| Trigger event | Raises buying probability |

## Positioning implication

The Chicago MSP market is crowded. Competitors already market:

- managed IT;
- Microsoft 365;
- cybersecurity;
- vCIO/vCTO;
- law-firm IT;
- healthcare/HIPAA IT; and
- fixed-price support bundles.

Therefore, the company should not rely on “managed IT for small business” as differentiation.

The research strengthens the current advisory-led position:

> identify the operating and security baseline → remediate into a defined target state → document ownership and evidence → govern the environment over time.

Potential white-space positions to test:

1. **Assessment and remediation partner that can coexist with an incumbent MSP.**
2. **Microsoft security/governance modernization for organizations that have IT support but lack architecture.**
3. **Evidence-oriented technology baseline for insurers, clients, tax/accounting obligations, or regulated practices.**
4. **Security + workflow modernization, not security-only or help-desk-only.**
5. **Productized professional-grade baseline for serious microbusinesses below conventional MSP minimums.**

## What not to conclude

- 1.1 million Chicago small businesses are **not** the serviceable market.
- 898,000/916,000 nonemployers are **not** uniformly valuable prospects.
- A national receipt distribution cannot be assumed to describe Chicago exactly.
- Professional-association membership counts are not firm counts.
- Industry totals do not indicate technology readiness or buying intent.
- Cyber-risk statistics do not prove willingness to buy this company's services.
- Published competitor price sheets do not prove achievable company pricing.

## Immediate strategy recommendation for validation

Keep the current four-track launch experiment, but split the results by business size and economic intensity:

1. accounting/tax;
2. legal/regulated professional;
3. healthcare;
4. construction/field-service/property.

Within each, deliberately recruit:

- owner-operated / 1–4 user firms;
- 5–9 user firms;
- 10–49 user firms; and
- 50+ user firms where warm access exists.

The objective is to determine whether the company is observing **one continuous market** or **two distinct buying/economic systems**.
