# Competitive Landscape — Chicago

## Source and status

- **Status:** current competitor/market scan and strategic hypotheses
- **Date:** 2026-08-19
- **Scope:** Chicago/Chicagoland managed IT, Microsoft, cybersecurity, professional-services vertical IT, and adjacent advisory models
- **Caution:** competitor websites are vendor-authored. Their pricing and service claims are useful market signals but are not independent benchmarks or verified customer outcomes.

## Executive conclusion

Chicago is a **crowded managed-IT market**. The company should assume that generic claims such as “managed IT,” “Microsoft 365,” “cybersecurity,” “vCIO,” “24/7 support,” and “IT for law firms” are already commoditized positioning language.

The strongest opportunity appears to be **adjacent to or above commodity support**, not to enter as another undifferentiated MSP.

Potential differentiation to test:

1. paid assessment and remediation that can **coexist with an incumbent MSP**;
2. Microsoft identity/device/collaboration/security modernization for firms whose support provider does not provide architecture;
3. evidence-oriented security/governance baselines tied to insurer, client, regulatory, or professional requirements;
4. security plus workflow/integration modernization rather than support alone; and
5. a separate, highly standardized security baseline for serious microbusinesses that fall below conventional MSP minimums.

## Competitive layers

### 1. Full-service local MSPs

Typical offer language:

- help desk;
- monitoring;
- endpoint management;
- backup/disaster recovery;
- Microsoft 365/Azure;
- cybersecurity;
- networking;
- vCIO/strategy;
- vendor management;
- 24/7 response.

Representative current Chicago example: PSM Partners markets managed IT, Azure/Microsoft 365, backup/disaster recovery, 24/7 support, networking, cybersecurity, identity/access, CISO consulting, and strategy.

Source: https://www.psmpartners.com/chicago-it-company/

### 2. Vertical MSPs

Law is visibly competitive in Chicago.

Examples include:

- ArchiTechture, which markets a “virtual IT department” for law firms and bundles Microsoft 365/security and even lifecycle hardware replacement;
- CTS, which markets unlimited proactive support, 24/7 coverage, law-firm applications, cloud storage, cybersecurity, and compliance-oriented services;
- CTI Technology, which markets law-firm help desk, Microsoft 365/Azure, backup/disaster recovery, lifecycle management, security, MFA, email protection, and regulatory-readiness support;
- PSM Partners, which has a dedicated law-firm service position alongside its broader Chicago MSP practice; and
- GO Technology Group, which markets Microsoft 365/Purview/eDiscovery work to Chicago-area law firms and other organizations.

### 3. Security specialists / MSSPs

These compete around:

- MDR/SOC;
- vulnerability management;
- security assessments;
- incident response;
- compliance/GRC;
- identity security;
- penetration testing.

The company should avoid pretending to replicate deep specialist functions that are better delivered by qualified partners. Its likely advantage is translating security requirements into a practical Microsoft/customer operating baseline and coordinating specialist work.

### 4. Microsoft/project consultancies

These compete on:

- M365 migration;
- Intune/Entra;
- Azure;
- Purview;
- Teams/SharePoint;
- identity/security projects;
- cloud migration.

This is closer to the company's implementation capability, but many project firms disengage after the project. The company's recurring-governance architecture can be a meaningful difference if customers value continuing ownership.

### 5. Internal IT + co-managed providers

At the upper end of the 50–150-user target, the real competitor may not be “another MSP.” It may be:

- internal IT manager + specialist consultants;
- internal IT + outsourced help desk;
- incumbent MSP + security vendor;
- Microsoft partner + internal operations.

The company's wedge should therefore allow a **coexistence model**, not require wholesale provider replacement.

These are **seller-published ranges**. They may reflect the provider's preferred service scope, target account size, tool stack, margin, and sales strategy. They are not an independent market-clearing price.

They are still useful for one major conclusion:

> A serious one-person or two-person business can fall below conventional MSP minimum economics even when its security risk is real.

That supports testing a productized microbusiness offer separately from full managed services.

## Relationship to current internal pricing hypotheses

The repository currently carries managed-bundle hypotheses around approximately:

- $75–$100/user/month Core;
- $125–$150/user/month Pro; and
- $175+/user/month Enterprise.

Those ranges overlap the lower-to-middle end of published MSP pricing. That creates an important decision:

- if the company's managed scope is materially **lighter** than a full MSP, it must explain that clearly rather than appear merely cheaper;
- if it intends to include true full-service help desk/monitoring/security, the lower tiers may require careful cost/margin validation;
- if recurring governance is the core product, it may be better to price **accountability and review scope** rather than imitate all-inclusive MSP per-user packages.

The current Foundation project hypotheses ($6k–$9k Core, $12k–$18k Full, $18k+ Complex) should similarly be tested against actual buyer willingness, delivery hours, and incumbent provider conditions.

## Competitive wedge 1 — assessment without forced displacement

Many businesses already have someone who resets passwords, patches devices, or provides help-desk coverage. Requiring the buyer to fire that provider before receiving value creates unnecessary friction.

Potential offer:

> independent technology/security baseline → target state → remediation plan → execute selected high-value remediation → govern the architecture while the incumbent continues commodity support.

Advantages:

- lowers switching risk;
- opens 50–150-user organizations with existing MSPs;
- makes the company complementary rather than threatening;
- creates evidence before a broader takeover decision;
- aligns to advisory-first principle.

## Competitive wedge 2 — “supported but not architected” companies

A particularly useful ICP may be firms that already pay an MSP but still exhibit:

- weak Conditional Access;
- inconsistent device enrollment;
- excessive global/admin access;
- unmanaged external sharing;
- poor licensing alignment;
- no application/data-owner map;
- no tested recovery evidence;
- weak documentation;
- reactive projects;
- no executive technology roadmap.

This condition is easier to sell against than “your MSP is bad.”

## Competitive wedge 3 — evidence-oriented baseline

Traditional MSP marketing often emphasizes tools and response time.

The company can test a different deliverable structure:

- evidence of MFA/access-policy coverage;
- managed/encrypted endpoint inventory;
- privileged-account register;
- SaaS/application ownership map;
- backup/restore evidence;
- risk register;
- documented lifecycle and incident procedures;
- roadmap with owners and dates.

This makes the service legible to:

- owners;
- insurers;
- accountants/tax practitioners;
- healthcare practices;
- clients that send security questionnaires;
- counsel/compliance professionals.

## Competitive wedge 4 — security plus operational improvement

Small firms often resist security programs they perceive as pure cost. The company's integration/automation capability can create a paired value proposition:

> reduce risk **and** remove operational friction.

Examples:

- secure external collaboration + standardized client/project workspace;
- identity lifecycle + automated onboarding/offboarding;
- device governance + automated provisioning;
- secure document handling + approval workflows;
- application inventory + integration/automation roadmap.

This is harder for a commodity MSP or pure MSSP to reproduce.

## Competitive wedge 5 — microbusiness product

The likely buyer is not “every freelancer.” It is an owner-operated business that has:

- meaningful receipts;
- sensitive client/customer data;
- payment/wire exposure;
- cloud dependency;
- no internal IT; and
- enough risk to care about professional controls.

A viable offer likely requires:

- fixed supported architecture;
- remote-first deployment;
- automated discovery/evidence;
- narrow remediation menu;
- documentation template;
- annual review;
- no unlimited help desk;
- explicit exclusions.

The white space hypothesis is that this customer may be **too small for a $2k–$3k/month MSP minimum but too consequential to remain self-administered**.

## Competitor diligence framework

For each serious competitor, record:

- target employee/user size;
- verticals;
- geographic service area;
- Microsoft partnership/specialization;
- managed vs project mix;
- security stack;
- help-desk scope/hours;
- vCIO/advisory scope;
- published minimums/pricing;
- onboarding/project fees;
- contract term;
- support exclusions;
- compliance/regulated-market claims;
- customer reviews/case studies;
- channel partners;
- employee count and likely delivery capacity;
- whether they will co-manage;
- whether they sell assessments independently;
- whether they productize microbusiness service.

## Recommended competitive tests

1. Obtain real proposals from 3–5 comparable MSPs for a synthetic 10-, 25-, and 50-user environment where legally and ethically appropriate.
2. Interview buyers who currently use MSPs and ask what they pay, what they believe is included, and what remains unresolved.
3. Ask prospects whether they would buy an **independent assessment without changing providers**.
4. Test whether “security/governance Foundation” is understood as meaningfully different from MSP onboarding.
5. Test a microbusiness fixed-scope price against conventional MSP monthly minimums.
6. Track win/loss reason against named incumbents.

## Current conclusion

The market does **not** support a strategy of winning because Chicago lacks IT providers. It supports a strategy of winning because a subset of small businesses appears to lack **senior architecture, evidence, governance, and cross-system improvement even when they already have technical support**.

That is the more defensible competitive thesis to validate.
