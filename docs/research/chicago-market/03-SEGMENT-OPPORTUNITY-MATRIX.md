# Segment Opportunity Matrix

## Source and status

- **Status:** strategic scoring framework based on current market research
- **Date:** 2026-08-19
- **Decision status:** hypotheses for validation, not approved vertical commitments

## Purpose

Broad industry size is not sufficient to choose a market. This matrix evaluates whether a segment is attractive **for this company's specific operating model**.

## Scoring dimensions

Score each account and market from 1 (weak) to 5 (strong).

| Dimension | What a high score means |
|---|---|
| Economic capacity | Revenue/receipts and margins can support professional technology work |
| Security consequence | Compromise can create material loss, liability, downtime, or client harm |
| External pressure | Regulator, insurer, customer, professional body, or contract creates urgency |
| Cloud/Microsoft fit | Business depends on email, identity, files, collaboration, endpoints, SaaS |
| Workflow complexity | Multiple systems, manual processes, integrations, documents, approvals |
| Decision access | Owner/partner/executive can buy without enterprise procurement |
| Senior-IT gap | Little experienced architecture/security leadership internally |
| Recurring potential | Ongoing governance, security, licensing, automation, or advisory value |
| Channel density | Trusted associations/referral partners can aggregate prospects |
| Competitive white space | Company can differentiate from incumbent MSP/vendor market |

## Size bands are different markets

### Nonemployer / one owner

**Best when:** high receipts, sensitive data, money movement, professional obligations, cloud dependency, or significant contractors.

**Commercial implication:** productized security/continuity baseline rather than a full consulting motion.

### 2–9 users

Potentially one of the strongest bands for professional services:

- owner/partner remains directly accessible;
- security/IT often managed informally;
- business can have substantial receipts;
- enough identities/devices/applications to create real operating risk;
- conventional MSP bundles can still feel expensive or excessive.

The ABA data support this pattern in law: small firms retain direct control of technology decisions while spending materially more than solos.

### 10–49 users

Current strongest all-around hypothesis for the existing company model.

Benefits:

- sufficient complexity and budget;
- likely no true senior IT/security executive;
- strong implementation and recurring potential;
- still manageable founder-led sales cycle;
- growing external compliance/insurance/customer pressure.

### 50–150 users

High-value, but more mature competitive environment.

Likely wedge:

- assessment;
- security architecture;
- Microsoft modernization;
- governance;
- remediation;
- integration/automation;
- coexistence with internal IT or incumbent MSP.

Less attractive wedge: generic outsourced IT replacement.

## Account-level ICP score

A prospecting database should score the following fields where evidence is available.

### Economic intensity — 20 points

- annual receipts/revenue band: 0–8
- estimated gross margin / professional-service intensity: 0–3
- business longevity: 0–2
- project/transaction value: 0–3
- evidence of technology/professional-services spending: 0–4

### Technology intensity — 20 points

- Microsoft 365/cloud dependency: 0–4
- user/identity count: 0–3
- managed device count: 0–3
- number/importance of SaaS/business systems: 0–4
- external collaboration/subcontractors: 0–3
- workflow/manual-process complexity: 0–3

### Risk and external pressure — 25 points

- sensitive customer/client data: 0–5
- wire/payment exposure: 0–4
- regulatory obligation: 0–5
- customer security requirements: 0–3
- cyber-insurance requirements: 0–3
- business-continuity consequence: 0–5

### Organizational gap — 20 points

- no senior IT/security leader: 0–5
- owner self-administers critical systems: 0–4
- weak documentation/ownership: 0–3
- fragmented vendor environment: 0–3
- incumbent MSP is support-only rather than strategic: 0–3
- recent failed project/incident: 0–2

### Purchase probability — 15 points

- active trigger event: 0–5
- executive sponsor access: 0–3
- warm/referral path: 0–3
- budget/timeline disclosed: 0–2
- willingness to provide access/evidence: 0–2

**Total:** 100.

Do not automate a go/no-go threshold until interviews/pilots show which factors correlate with actual sales and margin.

## Segment archetypes

### A — Regulated professional practice

Examples: tax/accounting, healthcare, financial advisory.

Signals:

- external rule or contractual standard;
- sensitive data;
- client trust;
- high value per employee;
- owner/partner access.

Best message:

> Turn required security and operational safeguards into a documented, supportable technology baseline.

### B — Confidential professional practice

Examples: law, consulting, architecture/engineering.

Signals:

- valuable confidential work product;
- heavy email/document collaboration;
- professional/reputational stakes;
- limited formal IT.

Best message:

> Make the tools the firm already relies on secure, governed, recoverable, and easier to operate.

### C — Transaction / wire-exposed business

Examples: real estate, property management, construction, certain finance/insurance businesses.

Signals:

- invoices, wires, ACH, deposits, closings;
- frequent external email;
- vendor/client impersonation exposure.

Best message:

> Reduce the chance that a compromised inbox or account becomes a six-figure payment mistake.

### D — Distributed field operation

Examples: contractors, trades, field services.

Signals:

- mobile endpoints;
- subcontractors;
- project files/photos;
- fragmented access;
- shared mailboxes/accounts;
- rapid onboarding/offboarding.

Best message:

> Give the field and office one controlled identity, device, file, and access model without slowing projects down.

### E — Growing knowledge business

Examples: consultancy, boutique agency, design firm, association management.

Signals:

- 5–50 people;
- cloud-heavy;
- repeated manual workflows;
- no CIO;
- growth is exposing operating weakness.

Best message:

> Replace founder-administered technology with an operating system the company can scale.

## Vertical-selection decision rule

Do not choose a permanent vertical based on addressable count alone.

A vertical should become a preferred market only after it demonstrates:

1. repeatable access to decision-makers;
2. a recognizable trigger condition;
3. willingness to pay for assessment/remediation;
4. repeatable technical scope;
5. acceptable implementation margin;
6. recurring attach potential;
7. manageable professional/regulatory risk;
8. strong referral or channel loops; and
9. lower customer-acquisition friction than a generalist market.

## Current recommended validation portfolio

Allocate early interviews/pilots approximately as follows rather than committing to one vertical prematurely:

- 25% accounting/tax/CPA;
- 25% legal/regulated professional;
- 25% healthcare;
- 25% construction/field/property.

Within each track, deliberately vary headcount so the microbusiness hypothesis gets tested rather than accidentally excluded.

Record the same variables for every interview and prospect to permit cross-track comparison.
