# Founder Equity and Incentive Design

## Source and status

- **Status:** researched redesign proposal; not approved
- **Research date:** 2026-08-19
- **Purpose:** preserve the useful economic logic of the existing founder/sales/execution model while separating founder ownership, incentive compensation, tax status, securities compliance, and governance
- **Professional review required:** startup/business counsel, securities counsel as needed, and partnership-tax advisor before any award is promised or issued

## Bottom line

The existing idea is directionally useful but should be legally restructured.

Keep the concept that:

- the controlling founder should preserve control;
- sales should earn for measurable commercial outcomes;
- execution should earn for measurable delivery/value outcomes;
- people should not receive large permanent ownership merely for joining early;
- the company should preserve a future pool.

Change the mechanism:

> **Do not make founder equity and deal-by-deal incentive equity the same thing.**

Use one ownership system for true founders and a separate approved incentive plan for contributors.

## How common is the current model?

### Founder vesting

The common technology-startup benchmark is time-based founder vesting, most often four years with a one-year cliff and monthly vesting thereafter. Stripe Atlas describes this as the industry-standard founder pattern.

The company does not have to copy that pattern. The important lesson is that founder ownership is normally structured around continued founder service and company repurchase/forfeiture mechanics rather than issuing a new tiny ownership interest each time a project closes.

### Performance-based LLC incentives

Performance-based vesting is not unusual in LLC incentive plans. Profits-interest/incentive-unit plans can vest based on time, individual performance, revenue, EBITDA/profit hurdles, sale events, or other measurable milestones.

The company's **deal-earned concept therefore belongs naturally in the incentive-plan layer**, not necessarily in the founder-ownership layer.

### Deal-by-deal permanent ordinary membership

Issuing ordinary membership interests after every project is much less attractive operationally. It multiplies securities, tax, valuation, ledger, K-1, governance, transfer, and diligence events.

The economic result can be reproduced more cleanly with:

- a profits-interest/incentive-unit plan with milestone vesting;
- phantom units/unit appreciation rights;
- cash commissions or profit-sharing;
- periodic formal grants based on accumulated points/credits rather than a formal security issuance after every invoice.

## Recommended two-layer design

## Layer 1 — true founder ownership

Use actual founder units for the people the company intentionally wants as permanent owners of the existing enterprise value and governance bargain.

The founder documents should answer:

- initial percentage / units;
- capital contributed;
- pre-formation work credited, if any;
- voting rights;
- manager appointment/removal rights;
- service commitment;
- vesting or company repurchase right;
- what happens on voluntary departure;
- termination for cause / bad actor;
- death;
- disability;
- divorce or charging-order event;
- bankruptcy;
- attempted transfer;
- right of first refusal;
- company/member purchase rights;
- change of control;
- drag/tag rights if used;
- distributions and tax distributions;
- IP/confidentiality obligations;
- restrictive covenants where lawful and appropriate;
- dispute resolution.

### Suggested founder vesting benchmark

For a founder who has not yet earned the entire ownership grant, counsel should model:

- four-year reverse vesting;
- one-year cliff or negotiated credit for significant pre-formation work;
- monthly vesting after the cliff;
- company repurchase of unvested units at the legally agreed price on departure;
- limited acceleration, if any, on a change of control plus qualifying termination.

This is a benchmark, not a final term.

### Existing controlling-founder percentage

The existing 51% target can remain an open business term. However:

- it should not substitute for manager-managed governance;
- dilution modeling must show whether 51% means issued/outstanding, fully diluted, or voting power;
- future pool grants can dilute it unless the documents preserve a specified voting mechanism;
- outside financing can change the economics and control structure;
- some Illinois LLC actions have statutory consent rules that must be mapped by counsel.

## Layer 2 — contributor incentives

Contributors should not automatically become holders of ordinary founder units.

Use a defined plan with one of the following instruments.

## Option A — LLC profits interests / incentive units

### What they are

For a partnership-taxed LLC, a profits interest is generally an interest that participates in future profits/appreciation rather than current liquidation value. IRS Publication 541 distinguishes a profits interest from a capital interest and states that receipt of a qualifying profits interest for services generally is not a taxable event, subject to exceptions.

### Why it fits this project

A profits-interest plan can be drafted to:

- use non-voting or limited-governance economics where legally/tax appropriate;
- vest on revenue, project profit, customer acceptance, retention, or time;
- impose an economic hurdle so existing company value belongs to current owners;
- include forfeiture/call rights;
- make awards under one plan rather than improvising a new contract every deal.

### Why it is not “free equity” administratively

A true profits interest can make the recipient a partner for federal tax purposes. IRS guidance states that partners of a partnership, including members of an LLC taxed as a partnership, are self-employed rather than employees when performing services for the partnership.

Consequences can include:

- Schedule K-1 instead of W-2 for partnership service compensation;
- estimated taxes;
- self-employment-tax issues;
- possible differences in employee benefit eligibility;
- allocations/tax distributions;
- capital-account and valuation work;
- state filings for nonresident partners.

This makes profits interests best for a **small number of meaningful long-term contributors**, not every occasional freelancer.

### Required plan mechanics

If selected, counsel should build:

- Incentive Unit / Profits Interest Plan;
- class designation in the Operating Agreement;
- hurdle/liquidation-threshold methodology;
- valuation method at each grant;
- standardized Award Agreement;
- joinder to relevant Operating Agreement provisions;
- vesting schedule and milestone definitions;
- forfeiture/call rights;
- termination treatment;
- transfer restrictions;
- distribution and tax-allocation rules;
- tax distributions;
- protective Section 83(b) procedure if counsel recommends it for the particular award;
- securities-law representations/exemption record;
- capitalization ledger.

## Option B — phantom equity / unit appreciation rights

### What it does

Phantom equity can economically track a percentage of company value, appreciation, or a sale payout without making the recipient an actual LLC owner.

### Why it may be better for this project

This can preserve the user's desired “earn a percentage through results” psychology while avoiding some problems of adding many actual members.

Potential benefits:

- no voting/member status solely from the phantom award;
- no cap-table ownership until/if converted;
- no partnership K-1 merely because of the phantom award;
- easier use with shorter-term contributors;
- performance metrics can be deal-specific;
- simpler cleanup when a contributor leaves.

Potential costs:

- generally compensation rather than capital ownership economics;
- Section 409A and other deferred-compensation rules can matter depending design;
- payout timing and liquidity need careful drafting;
- accounting treatment differs from true equity.

This is a strong candidate for the execution pool if the founders want many contributors but few actual owners.

## Option C — cash commission / profit-sharing

For a sales contributor, cash commission or project-based profit sharing may be the cleanest initial instrument.

This is especially important because SEC Rule 701 is not automatically available to every independent salesperson. SEC guidance states that independent salespersons without an employment relationship are outside the Rule 701 consultant/advisor category, although facts can differ for de facto employment relationships and other bona fide professional-service arrangements.

If the sales person is a genuine independent outside sales agent, do not assume the company can simply grant securities under Rule 701. Counsel should identify another exemption if equity is used.

A rational launch sequence may therefore be:

1. cash commission for early sales;
2. documented milestone credits toward a future equity/phantom award;
3. formal equity only once role, relationship, tax status, and exemption are settled.

## Capital interests: use deliberately

A capital interest entitles the holder to a portion of current enterprise liquidation value. IRS Publication 541 states that a capital interest transferred for services generally creates compensation income when the interest becomes transferable or is no longer subject to a substantial risk of forfeiture, subject to the restricted-property rules.

Do not use ordinary current-value membership interests casually for a contributor merely because the cap table is currently small.

## Section 83(b)

Where restricted property subject to Section 83 is issued, a timely Section 83(b) election can be critical. Current IRS guidance states that the election must generally be filed no later than 30 days after the property transfer.

Build this as an operational control, not a sentence buried in an agreement:

- award date recorded;
- recipient gets written tax warning and deadline;
- form/instructions delivered immediately;
- recipient acknowledges responsibility;
- company stores evidence supplied by recipient;
- no one internally promises a tax result.

Missing a deadline is not something the company should discover during an acquisition years later.

## Securities law: every grant needs a path

A membership unit, profits interest, option, or similar ownership instrument can be a security. The SEC states that every offer and sale of securities must be registered or qualify for an exemption.

### Rule 701

Rule 701 can exempt certain compensatory grants by eligible private companies to employees and qualifying consultants/advisors. Current SEC guidance also imposes quantitative limits and additional disclosure above specified thresholds.

Do not use Rule 701 as a generic “employee equity exemption” without checking the recipient and plan.

### Regulation D / private placement

Other private-offering exemptions, including Regulation D, may be appropriate depending on the recipient and facts. Rule 506(b), for example, has purchaser, information, resale, general-solicitation, Form D, and state notice consequences.

### State securities law

Federal exemption does not mean state compliance disappears. Illinois has its own securities law exemptions/notice rules, and Michigan law should be checked for Michigan recipients.

### Required issuance checklist

Before each actual security grant:

1. identify issuer and exact security;
2. identify recipient and service/investment relationship;
3. document consideration/services;
4. approve grant under Operating Agreement/plan authority;
5. establish valuation/hurdle if needed;
6. identify federal registration exemption;
7. identify Illinois and recipient-state exemption/notice filing;
8. deliver required disclosures;
9. obtain signed award/subscription/joinder;
10. complete Section 83(b) workflow if applicable;
11. update unit ledger/cap table;
12. notify tax/accounting provider;
13. retain a permanent closing set.

## Proposed sales-pool redesign

The current `20% sales pool` can remain a planning reserve, but the company should not promise that the pool consists entirely of immediately issuable ordinary membership interests.

Possible structure:

- reserve up to 20% on a fully diluted planning basis;
- first-line compensation = commission/cash incentive;
- long-term sales leader = potentially eligible for an incentive-unit/profits-interest or phantom grant;
- individual cap remains a founder-approved business term;
- awards vest against objective collected-revenue or gross-profit milestones;
- customer refunds, bad debt, rescission, fraud, or material misrepresentation can reverse unvested credits under defined rules;
- no unilateral after-the-fact change to the metric for a completed measurement period.

## Proposed execution-pool redesign

The existing execution-profit concept is stronger than a pure “hours worked” formula and can be retained, but the formula needs more precision.

Define:

### Project Contribution Margin

`Collected client revenue`  
minus `refunded/credited revenue`  
minus `direct third-party/subcontractor delivery cost`  
minus `direct labor cost at the approved internal cost rate`  
minus `deal-specific licenses/tools not separately reimbursed`  
minus `sales commission attributable to the engagement`  
= `Project Contribution Margin`

Then define an **Execution Credit** system.

For example:

- each qualifying project produces a fixed number of execution credits based on contribution margin;
- credits are allocated using pre-agreed role weights or documented post-project contribution percentages;
- customer acceptance and collection are prerequisites;
- credits accumulate toward periodic vesting/grant tranches;
- actual legal awards occur quarterly or at defined thresholds rather than after every invoice;
- individual and pool caps apply;
- a published calculation procedure controls disputes.

This preserves the intended merit-based system while reducing legal-event churn.

## Attribution governance

Replace present “founder decides everything” language with a controlled process.

### Proposed standard

The Manager determines attribution **in good faith and consistently with the written plan and contemporaneous project records**.

Evidence can include:

- CRM opportunity ownership;
- signed SOW role assignments;
- time/delivery records;
- project-management records;
- deliverables;
- customer acceptance;
- collected revenue;
- approved project cost ledger;
- documented role changes.

### Dispute process

1. calculation statement issued;
2. participant has a short window to identify objective errors;
3. Manager reviews and issues written determination;
4. financial calculation disputes above a threshold may be referred to the company's independent accountant;
5. conflict-of-interest cases use a disinterested decision maker;
6. nonwaivable legal rights remain preserved.

This does not give contributors control of the company. It gives the company a defensible process.

## Departure and buyback rules

Any actual equity plan needs more than “vested equity is retained.” Counsel should decide:

- whether vested units remain outstanding after departure;
- whether the company has a call right on vested units;
- purchase price: FMV, formula, appraisal, book value, or other method;
- good-leaver/bad-leaver distinction;
- payment timing/installments;
- treatment of pending but unaccepted deals;
- treatment of earned but not formally granted credits;
- restrictive covenant and confidentiality survival;
- tax allocation cut-off;
- voting rights during a buyout dispute;
- death/disability estate process.

Keeping every departed operator forever as a minority member is not necessarily desirable.

## Dilution and the meaning of “cap”

Every percentage in the current model should specify its denominator.

Use terms such as:

- issued and outstanding units;
- fully diluted units;
- voting power;
- profits participation percentage;
- liquidation percentage above a hurdle.

A statement that someone has a “5% cap” is ambiguous unless it says whether future issuances dilute that percentage.

## Tax distributions

A partnership-taxed LLC can allocate taxable income to a member even when the company retains cash. The Operating Agreement should therefore include a carefully drafted tax-distribution policy.

Questions:

- assumed tax rate;
- federal/state/local components;
- whether distributions are advances against ordinary distributions;
- treatment of different owner states;
- withholding for nonresident owners;
- priority relative to debt/reserves;
- whether distributions are mandatory to the extent legally available.

This is particularly important if contributors earn profits interests but do not receive large cash distributions.

## Documents to build after the structure is approved

### Founder package

- Founder Term Sheet
- Operating Agreement
- Founder Unit Purchase / Subscription Agreement
- Restricted Unit / Repurchase Agreement or integrated provisions
- Founder IP / Confidentiality / Invention Assignment
- founder tax acknowledgement / 83(b) package if applicable
- initial written consents

### Incentive package

- Incentive Unit / Profits Interest Plan **or** Phantom Equity Plan
- form Award Agreement
- form Operating Agreement Joinder where applicable
- performance-credit policy
- valuation/hurdle memo template
- securities exemption memo/checklist
- award approval consent
- cap-table/ledger procedure

### Sales package

- commission plan or sales contractor agreement
- collected-revenue / clawback definitions
- opportunity attribution rules
- equity eligibility language that does not promise an unlawful/unapproved grant

## Existing examples worth studying with counsel

- Stripe Atlas founder equity terms for a clean illustration of standard founder vesting.

These are reference patterns, not documents to copy blindly into an Illinois LLC.
